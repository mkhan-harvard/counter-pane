Counterpane Duo

For a closed window

    Glass stops the hiss. It doesn't stop the rumble.

    A closed window blocks high-pitched sound well and low-pitched sound badly. Idling engines, bass and the steady hum of data-center cooling pass through almost untouched, and earplugs struggle with them too. That deep, droning sound is what keeps people awake.

    For people living next to data centers, it never switches off. Counterpane goes after exactly that sound, at the window where it gets in.

    Sleep with the city outside.

    How Duo works

    The glass is the speaker. Instead of adding sound to your room, Duo stops the window from passing the rumble in, so the whole room gets quieter, not just one spot.

    1. It listens

      A tiny mic on the outside of the frame hears traffic a fraction of a millisecond before it reaches the glass.

    2. It pushes back

      Two pucks, one on each sash, vibrate the glass in exactly the opposite way. A sensor in each puck feels what the pane is doing, 4,000 times a second.

    3. It learns your room

      During a 10 to 30 minute setup, a bedside mic learns how your room sounds. After that, Duo adapts on its own.

    What Duo does, in simulation

    15 dB less rumble at your bed.

    Averaged over ten everyday noises on a double-hung window, with noise arriving from directions the system never heard during setup. 10 dB sounds about half as loud.

      For windows that stay open

      Duo works through closed glass. For people who sleep with the window open, the same idea moves to the opening itself.

      Screen

      Your existing window screen, window open

      Two clip-on drivers move each half of your screen a few micrometres in time with the incoming sound. Air squeezing through the mesh creates a small push the other way, which cancels deep noise. Air, light and bug protection are unchanged.

      Slim Hood

      On the sill, window open

      A clear hood, 8 cm deep and 45 cm tall, stands in front of the lower window. Air comes in, turns up a 5 cm channel and enters the room over the top. A clear sheet with tiny holes soaks up the high pitches, and a speaker in the base cancels the low ones.

      Data-center package

      Slim Hood plus Screen

      For homes next to data centers, the two together take on the steady hum from both sides: the Screen goes after the deepest tones, and the Slim Hood quiets everything else. Operators and towns facing noise complaints are natural buyers for neighbor programs.

      Next: Screen Pro

      The quiet of the Slim Hood, built into a screen 1 cm thick. A few hundred active cells, each about 4 cm across with its own outdoor mic, inside mic and driver, cancel sound right at the opening. A thin panel with small holes behind them handles the highest pitches.

        In simulation, the cells cancel about 19 dB at 315 to 500 Hz, 12 to 15 dB at 630 to 800 Hz and 8 to 11 dB at 1 to 1.6 kHz across the room. Above 2 kHz they would need to shrink to 1 to 2 cm, so the panel takes over. It needs custom high-speed chips, which is why it comes after the first products.

        The research behind it

        Every number on this page comes from physics simulation built for this project. Prototypes come next.

        How it was tested

        • Window and room. A 3-D model of a window and a 3.6 × 3.2 m bedroom. The glass is modeled with more than 230 ways of vibrating, plus how it radiates sound into the room with first reflections from the walls. It matches the textbook mass law to within 1 dB from 100 Hz to 1.6 kHz.
        • Double-hung windows. Each sash is simulated as its own pane, and the sound from both is combined at the bed.
        • Control. The best achievable controller for each design, plus live adaptive tests, all with about half a millisecond of electronics delay.
        • Fair scoring. Calibrated with noise from some directions and scored on noise from directions it never heard, across ten sounds: idling bus, traffic, truck, motorcycle, car bass, data-center hum, car horn, siren, voices and drilling.
        • Open-window designs. Wave simulations of a vertical slice through the window, combined with the room model.

        What it found

          All models compared

          Each bar uses the measure that model is built for: rumble for Duo, deep sound for Screen, overall loudness for Slim Hood and Screen Pro.

          Limits

          • These are best-case simulations; real rooms will do somewhat less.
          • Duo's overall loudness changes little, because glass already blocks most high pitches. It is built for rumble and hum.
          • The Slim Hood and Screen Pro were simulated in 2-D slices. The data-center package combines two systems that haven't been simulated running together.

          What's next

          1. Build a one-puck prototype for about $45 and measure it at five or more spots in a real bedroom.
          2. Get quotes for small shakers, about 6 cm across, around 1 N of force.
          3. Pilot with households near a data center.
